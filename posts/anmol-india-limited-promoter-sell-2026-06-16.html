On June 16, 2026, Tilak Raj, a promoter of Anmol India Limited, sold 34.4 lakh shares at ₹11.28 per share, generating total proceeds of approximately ₹3.9 crore. This is the first recorded transaction by this promoter, meaning no prior sale or purchase is on the public record. The trade involved a substantial portion of the company's equity, representing 12.11% of the public float — the shares available for trading outside promoter control. This transaction is exceptionally large in context; it amounts to 4.8% of the entire market capitalisation of Anmol India, which is a micro-cap company valued at roughly ₹80 crore.

The analysis price on June 18, two days after the trade, stands at ₹12.13. This is 35.1% below the stock's 52-week high of ₹18.69, indicating that the promoter sold shares when the price was significantly off its peak. At the same time, the current price is above the 52-week low of ₹8.40. It is important to note that the trade date (June 16) and the price data date (June 18) are different, so the price context reflects market conditions two trading sessions after the event, not at the exact moment of the sale. The stock has moved modestly from the sale price of ₹11.28 to ₹12.13 in that short window.

Several characteristics of this transaction are unusual and merit attention. There is no prior transaction history for this promoter, making this a first-ever recorded event. The float impact of 12.11% is exceptionally high — selling such a large fraction of the freely traded shares in one go can temporarily distort market supply and signals a deliberate reduction of the promoter's stake. The trade is also a clear reversal of the typical holding pattern, as promoters generally do not sell unless they have a specific reason. The combination of these factors — first transaction, high float impact, and a significant percentage of market cap — creates what is termed signal stacking, where multiple warning signs appear simultaneously.

For a retail investor monitoring promoter filings, the key takeaway is that a promoter selling a large chunk of their holdings for the first time is a noteworthy event, especially in a micro-cap stock where liquidity is thin. The sale reduces the promoter's stake, which can change the ownership structure and perceived alignment of interests. However, this analysis does not make any recommendation to buy or sell. It simply presents the facts: a promoter executed a large, first-time sale at a price below the 52-week high, with high impact on the float. Investors should use this information as one piece of context among many when following a company's disclosures.
